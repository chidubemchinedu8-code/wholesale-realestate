# Negotiation, influence and the seller conversation

Read before any call with a seller, a buyer, or a contractor who matters.

This chapter is **evidence-graded**. Every tactic carries what the research
actually shows, and its limits. Where the popular version of a technique is
stronger than the evidence, the popular version loses.

---

## 0. The one thing that decides the outcome

**Preparation, not technique.** The strongest predictor of negotiated outcome is
your BATNA — your best alternative if this deal dies (Galinsky & Magee). A weak
BATNA produces worse deals than any tactic can rescue, and a *poor* alternative
is worse than none at all, because it anchors you to a low number (Schaerer,
Swaab & Galinsky, 2014).

Before any seller conversation, write down and do not change mid-call:

| Item | Meaning | Example (262 S Harris) |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | what you intend to pay | $62,000 |
| **Reservation** | walk-away maximum, in writing | $69,239 (seller MAO) |
| **BATNA** | what you do if this fails | two other 43204 leads at gate stage |
| **Their likely BATNA** | their alternatives to your offer | retail listing, realtor net ~$71K |
| **Their deadline** | what forces the clock | taxes, probate, vacancy, divorce |
| **Your trade menu** | 5 non-price variables you can give | closing date, earnest money, repairs, possession, price certainty |

If you cannot fill the BATNA row, **you are not ready to talk.** The repo's own
rule: `UNKNOWN` stops the analysis.

## 1. Anchoring — the best-supported tactic here, with a real limit

**What the research shows.** Across three experiments, whichever party made the
first offer obtained the better outcome (Galinsky & Mussweiler, 2001). In the
control conditions the first offer correlated .85–.93 with the final price.
A meta-analysis (Orr & Guthrie) put it at **r ≈ .50** across 19 results, which
means: **treat the direction as established, the magnitude as contested.**
A 2025 meta-analysis of 374 effect sizes found g = 0.42 overall, rising to 1.14
for *ambitious* first offers.

**Why it works.** Selective accessibility: once a number is on the table, the
other side retrieves reasons it might be right. Strack & Mussweiler (1997) showed
the same car was valued higher when the anchor was higher.

**Why it goes wrong.** Northcraft & Neale (1987) is the finding that should worry
a wholesaler: **professional real estate agents' appraisals were moved by the
listing price, even though the agents denied it.** Experts are not immune. You
are a real estate professional. Their asking price is an anchor aimed at you.

**How to break an anchor aimed at you** — the four documented focuses, and only
three of them work:

| What you focus on | Effect on their advantage |
|---|---|
| Their alternatives if this fails | **eliminated** |
| Their walk-away price | **eliminated** |
| Your own target (written beforehand) | **eliminated** |
| Their target | advantage persists |
| Your own alternatives | advantage persists |
| Just counter-offering | not shown to help — the weakest move |

**The limit nobody mentions.** An offer extreme enough reads as an insult or as
proof no deal is possible, and ends the conversation instead of anchoring it
(Galinsky). The optimal anchor is aggressive **and inside the zone of
plausibility.** For a wholesaler that means: your offer must be explainable from
the comps in one breath, or it is not an anchor, it is an exit.

**Precision.** Precise numbers beat round ones (Mason et al., 2013) because the
recipient assumes the number was computed. `$62,400` outperforms `$62,000`.
Never invent precision you did not compute — this repo's whole standard is that
a number is traceable.

## 2. Aspirational ranges — a specific, better-anchoring tool

Ames & Mason (2015): a **range** whose *floor is your target* ("$58,000 to
$63,000") is perceived as more flexible than a single number, while still pulling
the outcome to your anchor. It outperforms both a point offer and a range whose
midpoint is your target.

Rules:
- Your target is the **bottom** of the range.
- The top of the range must still be one you would honour.
- Never a range around your maximum. "Up to $69,000" is a confession.

## 3. Concessions — the part almost everyone gets wrong

- **Reciprocity is a rule, not a courtesy.** Every concession you make must be
  conditioned on one in return. An unreciprocated concession signals that more
  are available and devalues every later one.
- **Shrink your steps.** $8,000 → $4,000 → $2,000 → $1,000 reads as approaching
  a wall. Equal steps read as a stairway with no end.
- **Trade variables, not just money.** Price, closing date, earnest money,
  who pays which closing costs, repairs, possession, financing contingency,
  inspection period. A seller who will not move $5,000 on price will often move
  three weeks on timing. Non-price currency is where the deal is actually made.
- **Never bid against yourself.** If they say nothing, do not improve your offer.

## 4. The specific evidence against what this repo used to say

The old script told you to ask: **"What's the lowest you'd go to close
quickly?"** Delete that. It is the single worst question available:

1. It **invites them to anchor** the number, immediately after you anchored.
2. It **surrenders information** — you learn their floor, they learn you want
   speed.
3. It **frames you as eager**, which weakens every subsequent concession.

Replace with a question that extracts information without handing over the
anchor:

> "If we could remove the realtor commission and the repair risk entirely, what
> would make this worth doing for you?"

That asks for their *interests*, not their reservation price, and it costs the
seller's position nothing to answer.

## 5. Influence — Cialdini, honestly

Seven principles: reciprocity, commitment & consistency, social proof, authority,
liking, scarcity, unity (unity added 2016). Each is a mental shortcut, and each
has an honest use and a manipulation.

**The test this repo applies: does the shortcut point at something true?**
Fabricated social proof, invented scarcity and borrowed authority are **banned**.
Not on moral grounds alone — they are detectable, and in a business built on
repeat buyers and word of mouth they cost more than they earn.

| Principle | Honest use in wholesaling | Banned |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Bring the comps. Show your work. A real number, first, for free | A "gift" engineered only to create debt |
| Commitment | Get agreement on facts one at a time (condition, timeline, price reality) | Small yeses used to trap a large one |
| Social proof | Real closed deals, real buyer names with permission | Invented "I've got 12 buyers for this" |
| Authority | Actual comps, actual repair scope, actual track record | Borrowed credentials, vague "studies" |
| Liking | Genuine common ground; you are a neighbour, not a call centre | Flattery, manufactured similarity |
| Scarcity | Real limits: your offer expires, your capital is committed elsewhere, the realtor's net is real | Fake countdowns, phantom competing buyers |
| Unity | Shared interest: both of you want this house solved | Us-versus-them |

**Scarcity is the one most abused and the most dangerous.** Saying you have
another buyer when you do not is a lie that survives until the closing table and
then destroys the buyer relationship you need for every future deal.

## 6. The seller conversation, in order

**Stage 1 — earn the right to talk.** Do not lead with a number. Lead with
evidence you did the work: the comps, the condition, the honest repair range.
Most sellers have never had anyone tell them what their house is actually worth
after repairs. That is your authority, and it is real.

**Stage 2 — find the interest, not the price.** Ask what they want to happen, not
what they want to get. "Where are you going next?" surfaces the deadline, the
motive and the real constraint. Probate, taxes, a job move, a bad tenant.

**Stage 3 — anchor, once, precisely, justified.** A single number, at or just
above your target, in an aspirational range if the relationship is warm. Attach
one sentence of why. Then **stop talking.** Silence is not rude; it is the
mechanism working.

**Stage 4 — trade, never cave.** Every counter gets a question and a trade:

- "I understand. Help me see where that number comes from?"
- "I can't do $X, but I can do [closing in 12 days / all cash / as-is / your
  timeline]. Which of those matters most?"
- "If I get you $Y, would you sign this week?"

**Stage 5 — name the impossible, do not negotiate it.** If their number is
incompatible with the comps, say so plainly and early. "At $79,900 there is no
deal for me — the comps don't support it. If that changes, call me." That is not
a failed call. That is a preserved relationship and a call worth taking twice.

**Stage 6 — confirm in writing the same day.** The drafter's interpretation
frames the residual ambiguity. Whoever writes the summary first defines what was
agreed.

## 6b. The second negotiation: the end buyer

The assignment fee is negotiated too, and the seller-side chapter does not apply.

- **The buyer's anchor is the spread, not the price.** They will compute
  `ARV − repairs − your contract price` and call that "my profit". If you let them
  set that number first you are negotiating your own fee against their anchor.
- **Show the deal as a completed property, not a contract.** Present the ARV, the
  repair scope and the projected all-in. The fee is then a line item in a return,
  not a toll they are being charged.
- **The buyer's BATNA is the other deal you have, not the other deal they have.**
  A buyer with three other assignments in the same ZIP has real leverage. Know
  which buyers are actually short of inventory.
- **Do not reveal your contract price until the fee is agreed.** It is the one
  number that converts your fee into "money for nothing" in their mind.
- **Scarcity only if true.** §5 applies with no exceptions. A phantom competing
  buyer is how a buyer list of forty becomes a list of twelve.

## 7. Psychology that costs you money

- **Sunk cost.** A second call on a dead deal because you already spent six hours
  on it. The six hours are gone either way.
- **Anchoring on your own earlier offer.** If you opened at $62,400 you will
  defend $62,400 rather than re-derive it. Re-derive before every call.
- **Loss aversion on the fee.** Fear of losing the $10,000 drives offers that
  destroy the buyer's spread and the deal. The fee is the output of a good deal,
  never the input.
- **Reciprocity debt.** The seller who does you a small favour ("drive by the
  property for me") creates an obligation that shows up as a soft concession.
- **Time pressure from the seller's clock, not yours.** A seller's deadline is
  leverage *for them* only if they know you know. Ask when they need to be out.

## 8. What this chapter will not claim

- That these techniques are reliably worth a specific dollar figure in
  wholesaling. The underlying studies are laboratory and MBA-student
  negotiations, not distressed single-family homes with title issues.
- That high-pressure closers "work". The evidence for first-offer advantage is
  solid; the evidence for aggressive closing tactics producing durable
  relationships is not, and this business runs on repeat buyers.
- That any of this replaces the comps. **A great negotiation on a bad ARV is a
  fast way to lose money with confidence.**

## 9. Mechanical gate

Run `bash scripts/negotiate.py --check` before any seller call. It refuses to
print a talk track without a target, a reservation, a real BATNA, their BATNA,
their deadline and three trade variables. Same principle as the rest of this
repo: **no written BATNA, no conversation.**

**What the gate does not do.** It checks that the fields are present and
non-vacuous; it cannot check that they are *true*. Three plausible words satisfy
it. It is a speed bump against the real failure — starting a call because the
phone rang — not a verification. The comps are the only part of this repo a
machine can actually check, and that is why the ARV is the part that is gated
hardest.
