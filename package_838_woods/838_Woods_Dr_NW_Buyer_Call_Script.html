838 Woods Dr NW — Buyer Condition Call Script

Atlanta, GA 30318  |  Prepared 15 Nov 2026  |  Disclosure-based buyer call prep

Lead with accuracy, not reassurance. The seller disclosure identifies material roof, water, porch/floor, plumbing/sewer, pest, and repair items. Position this as a condition-sensitive opportunity for a buyer who will inspect and underwrite the work—not as a turnkey or cosmetic project.
Document-control issue to resolve before marketing or contracting: the supplied disclosure identifies the property as 838 Woods Drive Southwest, while this call is for 838 Woods Dr NW. Do not state that they are the same property. Confirm the legal description, parcel/APN, and contract address first. The disclosure was signed 4 Nov 2026; it reported the home vacant for one week at that time.

1. Three rules for a confident call

  1. Attribute every condition statement: say “the seller disclosed” or “the seller reported,” not “the house has been repaired” or “the issue is resolved.”
  2. Be direct about the material items: do not minimize an active kitchen-area roof leak, the tarp-covered porch issue, water-related floor work, or the prior sewer backup.
  3. Give the buyer a clear next step: written diligence, site access, qualified inspections, and repair bids. The disclosure is not an inspection report, warranty, contractor estimate, or representation that a condition has been corrected.

2. Sixty-second condition summary

Say: “Before we talk numbers, I want to be transparent about condition. This is a 1966 home and the seller’s November 4 disclosure supports a condition-heavy renovation profile. The seller reports an approximately 17-year-old main roof, repairs during ownership, and an active leak in the kitchen roof area. The back-bedroom roof was reportedly replaced about six years ago. There is also a disclosed back-porch foundation issue; the porch covering is described as bad and currently covered by a tarp. The seller further disclosed prior water-related floor work, a moisture-related hump in the living-room floor, a prior sewer backup, polybutylene plumbing, and water entry at the basement stairs when it rains. There are also broken windows and a hole in the main-bedroom wall. So I would want you to price this only after your own walk-through, roof/structural assessment, plumbing and sewer scope, and written repair bids. If that is your buying profile, I can help move the diligence forward.”

3. Full call flow — ready to use

A. Opening and buyer fit

Say: “Thanks for taking the time. I want to respect your underwriting process and give you the condition picture plainly. Before I walk through the disclosure, are you looking at this as a cash renovation, a rental rehab, or a resale project? And do you have a contractor or inspector who can visit promptly?”

Listen for: cash/financing status, renovation experience, target exit, inspection availability, decision-maker, proof-of-funds process, and timeline. A buyer who needs a light rehab or cannot inspect should not be positioned as a fit.

B. Set the expectation

Say: “I am going to separate what the seller actually disclosed from what still needs independent verification. I do not want to overstate repairs, condition, or scope. This is a property for a buyer who can inspect thoroughly and build a contingency for concealed work.”

D. Balanced items to state accurately

Say this without overselling it: “The seller did not disclose exterior flood/water intrusion, flood-insurance claims, known floodplain status, boundary disputes, CC&Rs/HOA, leases, hazardous-substance issues, or current code/permit violations. Those responses are based on the seller’s knowledge and are not substitutes for inspection, title, survey, insurance, or municipal diligence.”

The disclosure also says the property was not in a historic district, was not subject to an association, and was not leased. These are seller responses, not independently verified title, zoning, or survey conclusions.

E. Transition to the buyer’s underwriting

Say: “Given the roof, water, floor/porch, plumbing/sewer, and pest history, I would not ask you to price this off photos or a light-rehab assumption. If the location and exit work for you, the right next step is a walk-through followed by your own written scopes. What would you need to see or verify before you could make a clean decision?”

5. Questions to ask the buyer before ending the call

  1. “Have you completed projects with roof, moisture, framing, sewer, or repipe risk—not just cosmetic rehabs?”
  2. “Will you inspect with a GC, roofer, plumber, and sewer-camera vendor before finalizing your number?”
  3. “Are you underwriting a contingency for concealed damage behind floors, walls, roof decking, and the porch structure?”
  4. “What is your intended exit: flip, rental, or owner-occupant resale—and what condition standard are you targeting?”
  5. “Who must approve the purchase, and when can they walk the property?”
  6. “Can you provide proof of funds and perform to your stated closing timeline after diligence?”
  7. “Which open item is most likely to change your price: roof, structural/floor scope, plumbing/sewer, moisture, or something else?”

6. Required follow-up before a buyer relies on the opportunity

Priority inspections
  • General property inspection plus roof/decking/penetration assessment.
  • Porch, floor/framing, moisture, and structural assessment as indicated by inspection findings.
  • Plumbing review and sewer camera scope; identify all polybutylene and replacement needs.
  • Pest/WDO inspection and evaluation of the back add-on, basement, attic/ceiling, and moisture-prone areas.
  • HVAC operational inspection; electrical review appropriate for a 1966 home; environmental/mold/lead testing if the buyer’s inspector recommends it.
Documents to request
  • Roof repair/replacement invoices, warranties, photos, and any prior roof reports.
  • HVAC installation and 2023 service records; warranty and permit documentation if applicable.
  • Roto-Rooter invoice, sewer backup details, pipe/toilet replacement records, and any plumbing reports.
  • Details, invoices, photos, and permits for the kitchen-floor reinforcement, porch work, and water-damage repairs.
  • Insurance claim records for both tree events: loss dates, scopes, repair completion, and open balances.
  • Active Pest Control treatment records and any prior pest/WDO reports.
  • Current title, survey, tax/parcel record, legal description, utility confirmation, and the executed contract with all addenda.

8. Closing language

Say: “I appreciate you being direct about your criteria. To summarize, this is not being positioned as a clean cosmetic flip. The disclosure presents roof and rain-entry risk, porch/floor and moisture questions, prior sewer/plumbing events, and pest history that must be inspected. If you are comfortable taking it through that process, send me the items you need and your availability for a walk-through. After you have your own scopes, we can have a factual conversation about price and terms.”

Do not say:

  • “The roof is fine,” “the foundation issue is only cosmetic,” “the leaks are fixed,” “there are no water problems,” or “there are no sewer issues.”
  • “The seller will repair it,” “there is no lead paint,” “the septic answer is a typo,” or “the SW and NW addresses are the same.”
  • “It only needs $___ in repairs,” unless the buyer has independently inspected and accepted a written scope.
